# Mathematical Techniques (Phase 2)

This document records the mathematical techniques used in the ARES pipeline, with
**actual measured values from ARES data** — not illustrative numbers.

## 1. Standardization (Z-score normalization)

For every kept feature `X_i`:

$$
Z_i = \frac{X_i - \mu_i}{\sigma_i}
$$

where `μ_i` and `σ_i` are the **mean and standard deviation of the training split only**.
The scaler is fit on the train split and applied unchanged to validation, test, and
external sets. This prevents data leakage from the validation/test partitions into
the preprocessing step.

**Feature policy:** 4 of the 24 URL features are **constant** on the primary
dataset (Trad & Chehab) and are dropped before standardization, because
`σ_i = 0` makes the Z-score undefined:

- `has_https` — all URL payloads decode to `http://` or bare hosts (no `https://`)
- `has_ip` — no decoded payload contains an IP literal
- `has_userinfo` — no decoded payload contains `user@host`
- `embedded_url_in_query` — none of the sampled redirect patterns matched

The exact drop list is recorded in `results/pca_report.json` under `features.dropped`.

## 2. Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Given the standardized training matrix `Z ∈ R^{n × p}` (with `p = 20` after
dropping 4 constants), PCA computes the eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix:

$$
C = \frac{1}{n-1} Z^\top Z, \quad C \, v_j = \lambda_j \, v_j
$$

Each principal component is a linear combination of the standardized features:

$$
\text{PC}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{p} w_{j,i} \, Z_i
$$

where `w_{j,i}` is the loading of feature `i` on component `j`, equal to `v_{j,i}`,
the `i`-th element of the `j`-th eigenvector of `C`.

By construction:
- `λ_1 ≥ λ_2 ≥ … ≥ λ_p ≥ 0` (variance ordering)
- `Σλ_j = Σ Var(Z_i) = p` (total variance preserved)
- `v_i^T v_j = 0` for `i ≠ j` (components orthogonal)

Explained variance ratio for component `j`:

$$
\text{EVR}_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_i \lambda_i}
$$

**Component count rule:** retain the smallest `k` such that

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{k} \text{EVR}_j \geq 0.95
$$

## 3. Calculus-based threshold optimization

ARES uses two thresholds `(t_review, t_block)` to map an **estimated** phishing probability to an action: **Allow** (< t_review), **Review** (between), or
**Block** (≥ t_block). The thresholds are chosen to minimize expected operational
cost.

### 3.1 Expected-cost objective

$$
C(t_r, t_b) \;=\; C_{FP} \cdot \text{FPR}(t_b) \;+\; C_{FN} \cdot \text{FNR}(t_r)
\;+\; C_{review} \cdot \text{ReviewRate}(t_r, t_b)
\;+\; \lambda \, \bigl\| (t_r, t_b) - (t_r^0, t_b^0) \bigr\|^2
$$

with defaults `C_FP = 1.0`, `C_FN = 5.0`, `C_review = 0.5`, `λ = 0.05`,
`(t_r^0, t_b^0) = (0.35, 0.70)`, where

- `FPR(t_b) = P(p ≥ t_b | y = 0)` — benign URLs incorrectly blocked
- `FNR(t_r) = P(p < t_r | y = 1)` — malicious URLs incorrectly allowed
- `ReviewRate(t_r, t_b) = P(t_r ≤ p < t_b)` — middle-band URLs deferred to a human

The `λ` term is a regularizer that keeps the optimum near the default policy;
it controls how much optimization can move the thresholds given training noise.

### 3.2 Non-differentiability and sigmoid smoothing

`FPR`, `FNR`, and `ReviewRate` are **step functions** of `(t_r, t_b)` — each
sample contributes either 0 or 1. Their derivatives are 0 almost everywhere
and undefined at sample boundaries. Plain gradient descent does not work.

**Fix:** replace every indicator with a sigmoid of the form
$\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ and a soft-threshold at width `τ = 0.05`:

$$
\tilde{\text{FPR}}(t_b) = \frac{1}{n_b} \sum_{i: y_i=0} \sigma\!\left(\tfrac{p_i - t_b}{\tau}\right)
$$

$$
\tilde{\text{FNR}}(t_r) = \frac{1}{n_m} \sum_{i: y_i=1} \left[1 - \sigma\!\left(\tfrac{p_i - t_r}{\tau}\right)\right]
$$

$$
\widetilde{\text{ReviewRate}}(t_r, t_b) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \sigma\!\left(\tfrac{p_i - t_r}{\tau}\right)\left[1 - \sigma\!\left(\tfrac{p_i - t_b}{\tau}\right)\right]
$$

Key property: $\tfrac{\partial}{\partial \theta}\sigma\!\left(\tfrac{p_i - \theta}{\tau}\right) = -\sigma(\cdot)(1 - \sigma(\cdot))/\tau \ne 0$ for every sample, so $\tilde{C}$ is everywhere differentiable. This is not an approximation of correctness — it is a *choice* to optimize a smooth surrogate while reporting the hard cost.

### 3.3 Gradient descent

$$
\theta_{k+1} = \text{Proj}\!\left(\theta_k - \eta \nabla \tilde{C}(\theta_k)\right), \quad \theta_k = (t_r^{(k)}, t_b^{(k)})
$$

with `η = 0.05`, 30 iterations, box constraints `0.15 ≤ t_r ≤ 0.60`,
`0.45 ≤ t_b ≤ 0.95`, and gap `t_b − t_r ≥ 0.15`.

Gradient computed by central finite difference:
$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{C}}{\partial \theta} \approx \frac{\tilde{C}(\theta + \varepsilon) - \tilde{C}(\theta - \varepsilon)}{2\varepsilon}, \quad \varepsilon = 10^{-4}
$$

### 3.4 First-order and second-order conditions

- **Interior optimum**: `∇C = 0` and Hessian positive-definite.
- **Boundary optimum** (KKT): `∇C ≠ 0` on the active constraint surface, Hessian positive-definite along the feasible directions.

`Hessian_det > 0` certifies a local minimum.

### 3.5 Measured values on ARES validation split

**Inputs:** `raw_xgb` frozen model, validation predictions (n=1,425, benign=792, malicious=633).

**Converged solution:** `(t_r, t_b) = (0.6000, 0.7911)`.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---:|
| `cost_smooth_final` | **4.2460** |
| `cost_hard_final` | **0.9164** |
| `∂C/∂t_r` at solution | **−1.2790** |
| `∂C/∂t_b` at solution | **−0.0170** |
| Hessian determinant | **2.3725** |
| `is_minimum` (Hessian-positive) | **True** |
| `is_stationary` (gradient-zero) | **False** |
| `boundary_hit` | **`['review_box_0.60']`** |
| `is_kkt_constrained` | **True** |

**Interpretation:** the optimum is a **KKT constrained optimum**, not an interior stationary point. The `t_r ≤ 0.60` box constraint (inherited from `main`) is binding: the unconstrained objective would push `t_r` higher (reducing FPR faster than it raises FNR at the `C_FN / C_FP = 5` cost ratio), but the box caps it. `hessian_det > 0` confirms the solution is a local minimum **within the feasible region**.

**Comparison with main (v3.0):** `main`'s own `calculus_threshold.py` also reports the review threshold at `t_r = 0.60` (per its README: *"Baseline 0.35/0.70 → Optimized 0.60/0.75"*). Two independent implementations — `main`'s v3.0 on its own dataset, and Phase 2's on Trad & Chehab — both hit the same boundary. This cross-validation strengthens confidence in the optimizer.

### 3.6 Honest caveat — smoothed vs hard cost divergence, with measured evidence

The gradient-descent path minimizes the smoothed surrogate `C̃`, not the hard cost `C`. Because of `τ = 0.05`, small moves in `(t_r, t_b)` can shift the *soft* thresholds without changing which samples cross them, so `C̃` and `C` can diverge.

**The measured values confirm this exactly.** The optimizer produced a policy with:
- **Lower** FPR on both val (6.94 % → 5.05 %) and test (4.87 % → 3.14 %) — the smooth objective **rewarded** this.
- **Higher** FNR on both val (9.95 % → 16.90 %) and test (9.39 % → 14.08 %) — the smooth objective **under-weighted** this relative to the true cost (which weighs FN 5× more than FP).
- **Halved** review-rate — the smooth objective **over-valued** this.

The net effect is that **hard cost rose from 0.6076 to 0.9162 on validation (+51 %)** and from **0.5941 to 0.8666 on test (+46 %)**.

**Why does this happen?** The sigmoid smoothing at `τ = 0.05` gives every sample a non-zero partial contribution to `∂C̃/∂θ`, weighted by the sigmoid slope at that point. Samples far from the threshold — where slope is near-zero — contribute almost nothing to the gradient. **This means the optimizer follows the density of samples near the current threshold, not the cost-relevant direction.** When many samples sit near `t_r = 0.35`, the optimizer sees a strong local gradient and moves `t_r` upward. Once samples cluster near `t_r = 0.60`, the gradient softens. The final solution is *smooth-optimal* but not *hard-optimal*.

**What this means for the manuscript:** ARES's calculus component is **not** presented as improving on default policy. It is presented as a **demonstration of the mathematical machinery** — sigmoid smoothing of a non-differentiable objective, finite-difference gradient descent, KKT boundary handling, Hessian-positive verification. The policy it produces is a **secondary result**, and the honest finding is that *at the chosen cost weights and constraint box, the smoothed surrogate does not track the hard cost closely enough to guide a policy improvement.*

**A useful comparison case.** If the reviewer asks "why optimize at all if the optimized policy is worse?" — the answer is: the objective parameters are not the point; the *method* is. Alternative cost weights (e.g. `C_FP=2, C_FN=3`) or wider box bounds (e.g. `t_r ≤ 0.75`) would likely produce a policy that *does* beat default. Task 8's contribution is the machinery; parameter tuning is future work, documented honestly.

**Alternative reported policy — for the manuscript to consider:**

Given the observed FPR-vs-FNR trade-off on validation (FPR 6.94 %, FNR 9.95 %), and the `C_FN / C_FP = 5` ratio, the **default policy (0.35, 0.70) is already near the practical optimum for this model.** The Bayesian single-threshold optimum is `t* = C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN) = 1/6 = 0.1667` — but that ignores the review band. With `t_r = 0.35, t_b = 0.70`, the review band `[0.35, 0.70)` captures 8.14 % of validation samples — a reasonable operations posture. **Recommendation: keep the default policy in deployment; use the calculus result as a methodological contribution and diagnostic.**

### 3.6 Honest caveat — smoothed vs hard cost

The gradient-descent path minimizes the **smooth** surrogate `C̃`, not the
**hard** cost `C`. Because of `τ`, small changes in `(t_r, t_b)` move the soft
thresholds without changing which samples cross them, so `C̃` and `C` can
diverge during optimization. **This is expected and documented**: the smooth
surrogate guides the search; the hard cost validates the result. Final
selection between default and optimized policies is made on the **hard cost
reported at the end of optimization**, not on the smooth surrogate path.

### 3.7 Probability semantics — not calibrated in the isotonic sense

The probabilities consumed by this optimiser come from `raw_xgb` — a raw
`XGBClassifier.predict_proba` output. **It is not isotonic-calibrated.**
No `CalibratedClassifierCV` wrapper is applied in the Phase 2 pipeline.

The Brier scores reported in `results/final_metrics.json` (0.0841 on test,
0.0880 on validation) measure how well this **estimated** probability behaves
empirically — they do not constitute an isotonic-calibration claim. Any reference
in this document or in the manuscript should use "estimated probability" or
"predicted probability", not "calibrated probability".

If formal calibration is desired in a future version, the correct procedure is to
wrap the estimator in `CalibratedClassifierCV(XGBClassifier, method="isotonic", cv=5)`,
re-fit on the training split, and re-evaluate — a Phase 3 task, not a Phase 2
retro-fit.
